Same burger, same cook, same sauce. The difference isn't the beef — it's the market structure: the kind of competition surrounding the seller.

The four questions on the order pad

Economists name any market by asking the same four questions. Memorize these and you can classify anything.

Smitty's order pad

1. How many sellers? A ton, many, a few, or just one?

2. Same product or different? Identical (a pound of ground beef) or unique (nobody else has our chili sauce)?

3. How easy is it to open up shop? Can someone start tomorrow, or are there walls in the way? These walls are called barriers to entry.

4. Who controls the price? Does the seller set it, or does the market hand it over?

Question 4 is really questions 1, 2, and 3 added together.

Stop 1: Pure competition

Smitty's buys ground beef. Three hundred suppliers sell the exact same grade, and every pound is interchangeable.

Sellers: very many Product: identical Entry: easy Price power: none

One supplier asks $4.10 when everyone else charges $4.00. Smitty buys from literally anyone else, and that supplier sells nothing.

Price taker. The market announces the price; the seller accepts it. That's the whole job.

Close real examples: wheat, corn, currency exchange, fish at the dock. Perfect purity is rare — treat it as the far left end of the dial.

Understanding check 1

A farmer selling sweet corn at the state market raises her price 30 cents a bushel. What happens?

Her corn is identical to everyone else's and there are hundreds of sellers. What would you do as a buyer?

Stop 2: Monopolistic competition

This is where Smitty's Chill Grill actually lives. Water Street has Smitty's, Burger Barn, El Fuego, Wing Shack, and a food truck. All sell food. None sell the same food.

Sellers: many Product: differentiated Entry: fairly easy Price power: a little

The magic word: differentiation

The chili sauce, the neon sign, the playlist, the free refills — these make people choose Smitty's even at 75 cents more. That is real price power, but it's small. Try $18 and everyone eats at Wing Shack.

Why the name confuses people: "monopolistic" sounds enormous. It only means each seller has a tiny monopoly on its own version. Nobody else can sell a Smitty's Double Chill.

Real examples: restaurants, salons, clothing brands, gyms, sneakers.

Understanding check 2

Smitty's charges $8.50 while Burger Barn charges $7.75 — and Smitty's still has a line out the door. Why?

Think about the chili sauce and the atmosphere. What was the magic word on the last page?

Stop 3: Oligopoly

Smitty's needs soda syrup. Three companies supply the entire state. Whatever Smitty's does, it's buying from one of those three.

Sellers: a few big ones Product: identical or differentiated Entry: hard Price power: large

The key word: interdependence

Each of the three watches the other two constantly. Supplier A cuts its price and B and C know within a day and cut theirs too. That's a price war, and nobody wins it.

So they often hold prices high instead. If they actually meet and agree to do it, that's collusion, and it's illegal in the United States.

Test tip: if firms are reacting to each other's moves, it's an oligopoly. No other structure behaves that way.

Real examples: airlines, cell carriers, soda, game consoles, large banks.

Understanding check 3

Smitty's syrup supplier cuts prices Monday. By Tuesday the other two have matched it exactly. What does that tell you?

Which is the only structure where firms plan around each other's decisions?

Stop 4: Monopoly

Smitty's wins the contract to be the only food vendor inside the stadium. Twenty thousand hungry people. One grill.

Sellers: one Product: no close substitute Entry: blocked Price power: greatest

Price maker — with a leash

Smitty's now sets the price instead of taking it. But charge $40 and people eat before the game or bring nothing at all. Even a monopolist can't force anyone to buy. Demand is the limit.

What blocks the door?

  • A government license or exclusive contract (the stadium deal)
  • Control of a resource nobody else can get
  • A patent on the product
  • Startup costs so huge nobody can enter — like a water company laying pipe to every house

Understanding check 4

A monopoly has the most price power of any structure. What still limits how high it can go?

There are no rival sellers, so whatever limits the price has to come from the buyer's side of the market.

Price taker vs. price maker

Price taker

The market hands you a price. Your product is identical to everyone else's and there are far too many sellers to matter. Pure competition only.

Price maker

You choose the price, within limits. The more different or the more alone you are, the more you choose.
Monopolistic competition (a little) → oligopoly (a lot) → monopoly (the most).

When a question asks how much control a firm has over price, it is really asking you to name the structure.

Understanding check 5

Smitty's opens a second location in a mall food court with eleven other restaurants, all selling different kinds of food. Which structure?

Count the sellers first, then ask whether the products are identical or different.

Three mix-ups that cost points

1. "Monopolistic competition means monopoly"

It doesn't. Monopolistic competition has many sellers. A monopoly has one. The names are similar on purpose.

2. Judging by company size

A huge company is not automatically a monopoly. Ask how many competitors exist, not how much money the firm makes.

3. Skipping the entry question

Barriers to entry separate oligopoly from monopolistic competition faster than anything else. Anyone can open a taco truck. Nobody casually starts an airline.

Understanding check 6

Four companies in the country make commercial fryers. Starting a fifth would cost hundreds of millions, and when one raises prices the others follow. Which structure?

Three clues are stacked here: how few sellers, how expensive entry is, and the price-following.

Say it in one breath

Pure competition: very many sellers, identical product, no price power.

Monopolistic competition: many sellers, differentiated product, a little price power.

Oligopoly: a few big sellers watching each other, hard to enter, a lot of price power.

Monopoly: one seller, no substitute, entry blocked, the most price power — limited only by demand.
